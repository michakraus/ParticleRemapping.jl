Comparison and open questions ​

The four constructions answer the same question with different choices of the canonical map and of the new weights (the constructions). This chapter sets them side by side: what each moves, what it keeps exactly, what it needs, and where it stops. It does not rank them; each table row states a condition or a property, and which one matters depends on the simulation that uses the redistribution. The overview page of each chapter shows its remap with the same pictures (the standard pictures S1, S2, A1 and T1), each on the example of its chapter. A section on higher dimensions and the open questions of all chapters close the chapter.

The rows follow the obstructions. The gauge rezoning and the transport with the identity keep every position and every sign of , so the empty quadrants stay empty. The projection with equal weights moves every marker by one area-preserving map: it fills the quadrants by folding the occupied region, not by shrinking the empty one, and it needs a density that is positive on its whole box. The sliced redistribution couples the markers and fills the quadrants with any density, but its weights follow a kernel estimate of the input, not a given density. The spline-projection redistribution couples the markers too, but its relaxation and its step fit 16 markers; its reduced runs are on its experiments page. Limits of each construction ​

Each chapter derives its own limits on its page Properties, limitations and higher dimensions, with the known issues of its code and its open questions; this list collects the main limits.

  • Hamiltonian particle projection. The fit can reduce its residual only where the old weights vary (weight-dependent Hamiltonians); with separable stages, a kick that compresses the markers at one velocity expands them at the opposite one, which the non-separable stages avoid. The target weights must be positive, which fails for hats finer than the marker spacing. Details: limitations.

  • Spline-projection redistribution. The step reaches its targets on the identity-connected branch when the contraction condition holds; the verification case violates the sufficient bound and uses substeps (the parameter solver). The defaults fit the 16-marker verification set-up; the pages the verification set-up, hole filling and the standard experiments show the failures outside it (K2, K3). Details: limitations.

  • Gauge rezoning. It moves the velocities along rays only and never moves a position, so it cannot fill a hole in . Its solution branch ends at a fold of the homotopy (one constraint in closed form). The package solves the Schur complement as a dense matrix (K9). Details: limitations.

  • Canonical transport. A consistent weight field exists if and only if preserves the sign-resolved current profile, and a smooth canonical map cannot, in general, make the marker distribution uniform (the limit of equidistribution). A folded sheet is treated one layer at a time (the fold). Details: limitations.

  • Sliced redistribution. The map is canonical for frozen slices and multipliers, and the multipliers come from the state. The squeeze can move markers far from their anchors (K6), and each lift solves a dense   system (K7, cost). Details: limitations.

Every construction computes something from the markers and is symplectic for its frozen value, except the gauge rezoning, whose gauge step is symplectic for any stretch (parameters that depend on the state).

The two constructions of the gauge-rezoning chapter also combine: the current-quantile matching gives targets for the gauge rezoning (combining the two constructions).

Open questions ​

Repeated remaps and state dependence ​

  • The long-time behaviour of a simulation with repeated remaps, given that a state-dependent remap breaks the symplecticity of the composite map (two readings). The composition of a push and the projection map is symplectic as a sequence of maps for fixed weights and parameters, but not as one map of the initial state.

  • A choice of the sliced multipliers that follows the density and keeps the sliced map symplectic as a map of the state, for example from a generating function (multipliers that depend on the state), or more generally a variational closure that matches targets.

  • The injectivity of the sliced map with slices and anchors read from the state, across the sets where two markers swap their order (slices that depend on the state).

  • Whether the push conserves : the redistributions conserve it exactly, the push depends on the field discretisation.

Targets and weights ​

  • The choice of admissible generators of the canonical transport that reduce clustering most, and how much such a remap gains, within the limit of equidistribution.

  • Folded sheets, where the matching must split between the layers (the fold).

  • The target marker density: an equidistribution is one choice. A density   with    moves the fold outwards, to   for a Maxwellian, and reduces the spread of the weights.

  • The projection of the weights: a dynamical low-rank density at   gives a small relative error in the tails in the test after a push, at rank 13 or less, in a weak, fixed field in  . Open are the rank that a self-consistent field and   need, a truncation that bounds the relative error in the tails, and the sign of , which the test never saw negative.

  • Charge against filling: the charge-conserving reweighting keeps the grid charge of the markers before the map, with the charge deficit of a hole, and lowers the fill (from to in the hole-filling experiment). Which charge is the right reference depends on whether the hole is a defect of the sampling or a feature of . The correction keeps the grid charge only.

Solvers and stages ​

  • The non-separable stages end at the iteration limit of their fixed-point solver, so the solvability of the midpoint equation limits the step; a Newton solver, more stages with smaller steps, or a composition with kicks and drifts may relax this. One application of the non-separable stages takes 0.16 to 0.19 s against 0.004 to 0.007 s for the kicks and drifts in the run of the script.

  • The coarse-basis regime of the spline-projection redistribution at large : with    the regularised parameter solve reaches the targets only approximately, and the velocities are recovered along the branch of the fold. The package tests the recovery on 16 markers; it has no experiment of this regime at large .

Higher dimensions ​

  • The current-quantile matching in several velocity dimensions: the matching holds along rays in the continuum (higher dimensions); a discrete matching with direction bins, and the admissible canonical maps in dimensions, are open.

  • A sparse solve of the Schur complement of the gauge rezoning on nodes, and its iteration count (K9).

  • The tensor product of the non-separable stages has parameters per stage, which is too many for  ; a sparse or low-rank parametrisation is needed there.

  • The Poisson problem of the density-interpolation flow lives on a grid in dimensions,   nodes in 3d3v with the hats of the experiment, and the package solves it with a dense pseudo-inverse (K10) (higher dimensions).

  • SplineRedistribution implements   only, while the statements of its chapter hold in dimensions; its pairwise basis has    parameters in 3d3v with the defaults, so the regime of exact targets is out of reach there, and the coarse collective basis is untested at large (higher dimensions).

  • Correlations between the axes: the per-axis sliced map sees the two-dimensional marginals only (per-axis version); lifts conditioned on a few chosen coordinates or lifts in rotated frames lie between the per-axis map and a full conditioning with slices (higher dimensions).

Tests ​

  • A dynamical test: the holes of the projection experiments are cut out by hand, and the push of the test after a push only shears them. A test on holes that a simulation produces, for example in a bump-on-tail instability, is open for every construction.

  • The mean-field limit of a one-slice quantile lift, and the rate at which slices of finite size escape the obstruction of uncoupled maps (coupling is necessary, not sufficient).
